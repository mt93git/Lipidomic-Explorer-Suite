# 1.1 INTRODUCTION

## (untitled panel)
Nearly all the commercially important fats and oils of animal and plant origin contain mainly (>95%) the simple lipid class – triacylglycerols (‘triglycerides’), which are chemically inert, highly hydrophobic high energy density. This includes all the vegetable oils, such as those from corn (maize), olive, palm and sunflower, and animal fats, such as tallow, lard and butter, as well as manufactured products such as margarines. The more abundant animal triacylglycerols are depots fats from adipose tissue or milk fats, where their main function in vivo is as a store of energy and perhaps of bioactive lipids, but it is increasingly being recognized that triacylglycerols in most tissues, but especially those in plasma or liver, have a more dynamic role in cellular metabolism. Similarly, seed oils serve as a source of energy and structural fatty acids for the developing plant embryo. In this web page, the fatty acid compositions of triacylglycerols together with their organization within molecules in various organisms are described. Two companion documents on this website are Triacylglycerols: Part 2. Biosynthesis and metabolism and Triacylglycerols: Part 3 – Regio- and stereospecific analysis procedures.

## 1.1 INTRODUCTION
Triacylglycerols (TAGs) consist of the trihydric alcohol glycerol esterified mainly with long-chain fatty acids (C14 to C22). When the two primary hydroxyl groups are esterified with different fatty acids, the resulting triacylglycerol can display chiral asymmetry and thus be optically active, although this is usually too low to be measured.

The stereochemistry of TAGs and other glycerolipids is described by the “stereospecific numbering” (sn) system recommended by the IUPAC–IUB Commission.

## Key points
- Natural glycerol is of L-configuration.
- The secondary (middle) carbon is C-2 (β-position).
- The ends are C-1 (α-position) and C-3 (α′-position).
- Triacyl-sn-glycerol is the recommended term.
- “Triglyceride” is still used industrially and clinically, but not recommended for scientific studies.

### Nomenclature and positional terminology
- When detailed stereochemistry is not specified, the positions are termed α (sn-1), β (sn-2) and α′ (sn-3).
- The prefix sn is placed before the stem name of the compound (e.g., triacyl-sn-glycerol).

## Why stereospecific distribution matters
- Position sn-2 fatty acid is released as 2-monoacylglycerol on digestion and is absorbed intact.
- Different positions influence physical properties (melting point, crystal structure, digestibility).
- Distribution patterns reflect biosynthetic pathways and can be characteristic of fats and oils.

## HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT OF ANALYSIS
Differences in the distributions of fatty acids among the positions of TAGs were first demonstrated by enzymatic hydrolysis procedures, mainly pancreatic lipase hydrolysis for analysis of sn-2 fatty acids (regiospecific analysis), before stereospecific hydrolysis procedures were developed that permitted all three positions to be determined (see Part 3 of this topic).

## KEY TAKE-HOME MESSAGES
- Triacylglycerols are the predominant lipids (>95%) in most commercial fats and oils.
- The sn (stereospecific numbering) system is essential for accurate description of TAG structure and function.
- sn-1,2-Diacylglycerols are key intermediates and signalling molecules (described in Parts 2 and 3).
- Fatty acid composition and positional distribution determine physical properties, nutrition and metabolic fate.
- Understanding TAG structure is central to lipid science, nutrition, health and industry.

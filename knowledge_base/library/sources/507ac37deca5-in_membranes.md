# 2. CHOLESTEROL IN MEMBRANES

## (untitled panel)
Experiments with mutant cell lines and specific inhibitors of cholesterol biosynthesis suggest that an equatorial hydroxyl group at C-3 of sterols is essential for the growth of mammalian cells. The Δ5 double bond ensures that the molecule adopts a planar conformation, and the flexible iso-octyl side chain and the C-18 methyl group ensure proper orientation of the sterol. Plant sterols can substitute for cholesterol in supporting many of the bulk membrane properties in vitro, but cholesterol per se is essential for innumerable other purposes.

- Four fused rings (A–D) are essentially planar.
- 3β-hydroxyl group is equatorial.
- Double bond between C-5 and C-6.
- Methyl groups at C-18 and C-19 are β-oriented (above plane); iso-octyl side chain at C-17 is flexible.
- The molecule spans ~half the bilayer (~15 Å).

## Key points
- Intercalates between phospholipids, spanning ~half the bilayer.
- Amino/α-face (down) is smooth → contacts saturated acyl chains.
- β-face (up) is rough → methyl groups at C-10 and C-13 project.
- Interactions: van der Waals, hydrophobic, and H-bonding (3-OH).
- Side chain aligns with saturated chains; poorly with unsaturated chains.

## 2. EFFECTS ON MEMBRANE PHYSICAL PROPERTIES
Cholesterol modulates membrane fluidity across a broad range of temperatures

Membranes rich in unsaturated lipids (liquid-disordered, Lo) + Cholesterol → Becomes more ordered (liquid-ordered, Lo)

Membranes rich in saturated lipids (solid gel, So) + Cholesterol → Becomes more fluid (liquid-ordered, Lo)

## Consequences of cholesterol in membranes
- Increases order and thickness of unsaturated membranes; decreases order (increases fluidity) of saturated membranes.
- Promotes and stabilizes the liquid-ordered (Lo) phase over a wide range of sterol concentrations and temperatures.
- Decreases passive permeability to small solutes.
- Allows membranes to bend and withstand mechanical stresses.
- Enables fine-tuning of membrane composition and organization for cell metabolism.

## Rafts and sphingolipids
Cholesterol forms stoichiometric complexes with saturated acyl chains of sphingomyelin and, to a lesser extent, phosphatidylcholine in lipid rafts, contributing to raft stability and signalling. It may also regulate sphingomyelin synthesis, particularly very-long-chain species.

## Distribution and functions in organelles
- Plasma membrane: highest level (30–50% of lipid); ~60–80% of cell free cholesterol. → Barrier function, signalling, endo/exocytosis.
- Early & recycling endosomes: enriched; role in trafficking and signalling.
- Late endosomes: little or no cholesterol.
- Endoplasmic reticulum: low (~5%). → Greater membrane flexibility for protein insertion and folding.
- Golgi apparatus: intermediate amount.
- Mitochondria: low overall; concentrated in nanodomains at regions of high curvature in the inner mitochondrial membrane.
- Brain: ~25% of total body free cholesterol; 70–80% in myelin; essential for synapse function and precursor of oxysterols.

## Cholesterol flip-flop and leaflet asymmetry
- Cholesterol flips rapidly between leaflets, but its distribution is asymmetric in many cells.
- Most evidence indicates more cholesterol in the outer (exoplasmic) leaflet of the plasma membrane.
- This asymmetry promotes negative curvature and facilitates membrane fusion (exocytosis) and signalling.
- Human erythrocyte inner (cytoplasmic) leaflet has >50% excess phospholipids, possible only with asymmetric cholesterol distribution.

## 3. INTERACTIONS WITH MEMBRANE PROTEINS
Sterol-sensing domain (SSD)
~180 amino acids; 5 transmembrane helices; binds sterols.
Found in many proteins involved in cholesterol homeostasis and trafficking.

G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs)
- Cholesterol binds directly or allosterically.
- Affects ligand binding, activation or oligomerization.
- Essential for β2-adrenergic, oxytocin and serotonin receptors (increases agonist affinity).
- Rhodopsin stabilized in inactive state by direct binding + membrane curvature effects.
- Serotonin1A receptor binds ~10 cholesterol molecules; senses via Lys in "CRAC" motif (TM2).

Ion pumps and transporters
- (Na+–K+)-ATPase and others have specific cholesterol binding sites.
- Cholesterol depletion in plasma membrane can inhibit ion pumps.
- These pumps consume high ATP and maintain ion gradients essential for cell metabolism.

## Other locations
- Cholesterol in nucleus associates with chromatin.
- Ends bind deep within protein cavities or at membrane-facing surfaces; H-bonding involved.

## 4. KEY ROLES OF CHOLESTEROL IN MEMBRANE FUNCTION
- Barrier function: Increases membrane thickness and reduces permeability to ions and small molecules.
- Membrane mechanics: Confers flexibility yet stability; enables bending, fission and fusion.
- Lipid organization: Drives formation of ordered raft domains with sphingolipids.
- Protein function regulation: Necessary for proper structure, localization and activity of many membrane proteins.
- Signalling platforms: Organizes receptors and effectors; controls signalling pathways.

## 5. SUMMARY
- Cholesterol is an amphiphilic sterol that intercalates between phospholipids in bilayers.
- It modulates membrane fluidity: orders unsaturated membranes and fluidizes saturated membranes.
- It lowers permeability, increases thickness and provides mechanical resilience.
- It is asymmetrically distributed, mostly in the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane.
- It organizes lipid rafts with sphingolipids and regulates proteins, including GPCRs and ion pumps.
- These actions are essential for membrane integrity, signalling, trafficking and overall cell function.

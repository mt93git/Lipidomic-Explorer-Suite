# 4. α-D-GALACTOSYLCERAMIDE (α-GalCer)

## (untitled panel)
α-Galactosylceramides linked through an α-D-galactosyl unit are potent activators of invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells by binding to CD1d on antigen-presenting cells.

## STRUCTURE OF α-D-GALACTOSYLCERAMIDE
Diagram labels: α-D-Galactosylceramide (α-GalCer)

R1 = fatty acyl chain (e.g., 16:0, 24:1, etc.)

## IMMUNE ACTIVATION BY α-GALCER
Diagram labels: Antigen-presenting cell (e.g., dendritic cell); α-GalCer loads onto CD1d; CD1d; Recognized by iNKT cell TCR; iNKT cell; Activation, proliferation and cytokine release (e.g., IL-10, IFN-γ)

## KEY POINTS
- Natural endogenous ligand: α-GalCer with a 24:1 fatty acid (in very small amounts) is presented by CD1d as an endogenous ligand for NKT cells in the thymus and periphery.
- Activated NKT cells produce cytokines (e.g., IL-10, IFN-γ) that modulate innate and adaptive immune responses.
- Synthetic α-GalCer analogues with straight-chain fatty acids are more efficient than branched forms (e.g., from B. fragilis).
- α-Glucosylceramide and α-psychosine analogues are also active in iNKT cell activation.

## SOURCE, METABOLISM AND STRUCTURAL VARIATION
### Probable Source
- Not certain to be synthesized in animal tissues.
- Likely derived mainly from gut microbiota (e.g., Bacteroides fragilis and relatives); also found in marine sponge Agelas mauritianus and in cow’s milk.
- Bacterial ceramide–galactosyltransferases have been identified that synthesize α-GalCer.

### Typical Molecular Forms
Mouse gut (dominant form)

2-R-hydroxylated hexadecanoyl (16:0) linked to C18-sphinganine

B. fragilis (common form)

Longer-chain fatty acids (often 20–24 carbons) with iso-methyl branches in the sphingoid base

## CATABOLISM
Concentration in tissues is controlled by a two-step catabolism:

Diagram labels: α-GalCer; 1. Acid ceramidase; Lyso-α-GalCer (sphingosine + sugar); 2. α-Glycosidase; Sphingosine; Galactose

### Decrease in α-GalCer
Observed in mice under stress conditions that alter the gut microbiota, including Western diet, colitis and influenza A virus infection; may impact systemic immune responses.

## THERAPEUTIC POTENTIAL
- Preclinical studies: effective against lung and colorectal cancers, melanoma, leukaemia.
- Safe and efficacious as anti-tumour immunotherapeutic agents and vaccine adjuvants against viruses and intracellular bacteria.
- Phase I/II clinical trials in high-risk melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer show promising results.
- Synthetic analogues with anti-viral properties under development.
- PLS-α-GalCer (scavenger receptor-A1 targeted prodrug) shows enhanced antitumour activity in mouse models.
- In a mouse model, α-GalCer improved resolution of neuroinflammation and cognitive performance by modulating inflammatory cytokines.

## SUMMARY
α-Galactosylceramides are microbial (and dietary) lipids that act as natural ligands for CD1d, activating iNKT cells and shaping immune responses. They are under active investigation as immunotherapeutic and vaccine adjuvant agents for cancer, infections and inflammatory diseases.

## 5. HYDROLYSIS/CATABOLISM OF GLYCOSPHINGOLIPIDS
Glycosphingolipids are degraded primarily in lysosomes by sequential action of exohydrolases, releasing monosaccharides and ultimately ceramide, which is further hydrolysed to sphingoid base and fatty acid for reutilization.

## THE LYSOSOME
Diagram labels: Limiting membrane (glycocalyx of N-glycosylated proteins + GM3); Internal lysosomal vesicles; Soluble acid hydrolases (pH ~4.5–5.0); Lysobisphosphatidic acid (bis(monoacylglycero)phosphate) in the inner membrane confers digestion resistance

- Sections of plasma membrane enter the cell by endocytosis and are transported via endosomes to lysosomes.
- The glycocalyx and GM3 form a hydrophilic barrier that protects the perimeter membrane from hydrolytic enzymes and prevents escape of lipids and their hydrolysates.

## LYSOSOMAL DEGRADATION PATHWAY
Diagram labels: Complex glycosphingolipids (oligoglycosylceramides, gangliosides); Exohydrolases (sequential removal from non-reducing end); Monoglycosylceramide; Ceramide; Monosaccharides released; Degradation of ceramide; Ceramidase; Sphingoid base (e.g., sphingosine); Fatty acid

### β-GLYCOSIDASES / β-GALACTOSIDASE
| Enzyme | Localization / Notes |
|---|---|
| GBA1 (β-glucosidase) | Lysosomal hydrolase (deficiency → Gaucher disease type 1) |
| GBA2 (β-glucosidase) | Ubiquitous non-lysosomal enzyme |
| GBA3 (β-glucosidase) | Cytosolic; found in kidney, liver, spleen and other tissues; function not fully clear |
| Lysosomal β-galactosidase | (One isoform) removes galactose from GalCer |

## REQUIREMENTS FOR LYSOSOMAL CATABOLISM
Glycolipids with fewer than four carbohydrate residues are embedded in intralysosomal membranes, while the enzymes are soluble. Hence, degradation requires:

Diagram labels: Negatively charged lipids (e.g., BMP, sulfatides); Activator proteins (saposin A, B, C, D); Water-soluble glycoproteins (low molecular weight)

These factors extract the glycolipid from the membrane into mixed micelles where enzymes can access and hydrolyse the substrate.

## OUTCOME AND SIGNIFICANCE
- Monosaccharides, sphingoid bases and fatty acids are released.
- These components are transported out of the lysosome for reuse in biosynthetic pathways or further catabolism.
- Defects in lysosomal hydrolases cause accumulation of specific glycosphingolipids, leading to lysosomal storage disorders (e.g., Gaucher, Sandhoff, GM1 gangliosidosis).

## KEY POINT
The lysosome is the central hub for the turnover of all glycosphingolipids, ensuring cellular homeostasis and preventing toxic accumulation.

# 6. CHOLESTEROL ESTERS

## (untitled panel)
Cholesterol esters (CEs) with long-chain fatty acids esterified to the 3β-hydroxyl group are hydrophobic and biologically inert. They are ideal for transport in plasma lipoproteins and for intracellular storage in lipid droplets to buffer excess free cholesterol. CEs do not contribute to membrane structure, but can accumulate in atherosclerotic plaques and steroidogenic tissues such as the adrenal glands.

## Biosynthesis of cholesterol esters
In most tissues, ACAT (sterol O-acyltransferase) esterifies cholesterol with fatty acyl-CoA. Two isoforms exist in the ER membrane.

Diagram labels: Cholesterol; Fatty acyl-CoA; ACAT (SOAT); Cholesterol ester; CoA-SH

## ACAT isoforms
- Isoform: ACAT1; Tissue distribution: Most tissues, especially macrophages, adrenal & sebaceous glands; Main function: Stores CEs in cytosolic lipid droplets; important in foam cells & cancer.
- Isoform: ACAT2; Tissue distribution: Liver and small intestine; Main function: Supplies CEs for nascent lipoproteins (VLDL, chylomicrons).

## Key features
- ACATs are ER membrane proteins with multiple transmembrane domains and a catalytic histidine.
- Prefer saturated and monoenoic fatty acyl-CoAs as substrates.
- Members of the MBOAT (O-acyltransferase) superfamily.

## Storage
- CEs are packed into cytosolic lipid droplets adjacent to the ER (e.g., in adrenal cortex).

Diagram labels: Perilipin; ACAT1; Lipid droplet (cholesteryl esters); Endoplasmic reticulum

## Esterification of cholesterol in plasma by LCAT (on HDL)
In plasma, LCAT transfers the fatty acid at sn-2 of phosphatidylcholine (PC) to cholesterol.

Diagram labels: ApoA-I; HDL; LCAT reaction (multi-step mechanism); Phosphatidylcholine (lecithin); sn-2; LCAT (activated by ApoA-I); Cholesterol; Cholesteryl ester; 1-Acyl-lysophosphatidylcholine

## Key points
- LCAT is a 49 kDa glycoprotein (≈60 kDa with glycans); made in liver; circulates bound to HDL.
- ApoA-I concentrates substrate and opens "lid" on LCAT to expose active site.
- Prefers C18 fatty acids (18:1, 18:2) in plasma PC.
- CE accumulation in HDL core → particle matures (discoidal → spherical).
- CEs are transferred to LDL and VLDL by CETP.
- Drives reverse cholesterol transport to liver.

## Significance
- Promotes efflux of cholesterol from cell membranes and macrophages.
- Essential for cholesterol homeostasis; deficiency → low HDL, corneal opacity, accelerated atherosclerosis.
- Target for therapeutic intervention.

## Oxidized cholesterol esters
CEs containing oxidized PUFA or oxidized cholesterol acquire biological activity.

Diagram labels: Oxidation; Oxidized cholesteryl ester (e.g., cholesteryl arachidonate oxides)

## Biological effects
- Present in oxidized LDL and atherosclerotic lesions.
- Bind CD14 (not CD36) → activates TLR4.
- Stimulate macrophages to release inflammatory cytokines.
- Hydrolysis releases oxidized fatty acids → incorporated into phospholipids → further bioactive lipids.
- Contribute to inflammation and atherogenesis.

## Sources of oxidation
- 15-Lipoxygenase metabolism.
- Free radical–induced lipid peroxidation.

## Hydrolysis of cholesterol esters
Cholesteryl ester hydrolases (CEHs) release cholesterol and free fatty acids when needed.

Diagram labels: Cholesteryl ester; CEH; Cholesterol; Fatty acid

## Major CE hydrolases in mammals
- Neutral cholesteryl ester hydrolase (nCEH): in macrophages, liver, etc.: important in preventing foam cell formation.
- Lysosomal acid lipase (LAL): lysosomes.
- Hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL): adrenal, adipose tissue.
- Hepatic cytosolic cholesteryl ester hydrolase (HCEH).
- Carboxyl ester hydrolase (CEH).

## Functions
- Provide cholesterol for membrane turnover and lipoproteins.
- Supply cholesterol for steroid hormone synthesis (e.g., adrenal glands).
- Prevent excessive CE accumulation in macrophages and tissues.

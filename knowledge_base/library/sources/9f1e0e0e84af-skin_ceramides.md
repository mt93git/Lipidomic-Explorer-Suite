# 5. 1-O-ACYLCERAMIDES

## (untitled panel)
Minor components of lipid droplets (LD) that may act as inert storage lipids to limit ceramide-mediated apoptosis.

- Found in Vernix caseosa: C11–C38 O-ester fatty acids, C12–C39 amide fatty acids and C16–C24 sphingoid bases.

## Lysylceramides
Ceramides in which the 1-O-acyl group is linked to the ε-amino group of lysine. Structural analogues of lysyl-diacylglycerols of Mycobacteria. Identified in anaerobic marine bacteria from the Black Sea.

## 6. SKIN CERAMIDES

## The skin barrier
Diagram labels: Stratum corneum (lipid lamellae + corneocytes); Granular layer; Spinous layer; Basal layer; Dermis (collagen, immune cells, blood vessels); Subcutaneous fat (thermogenic depots)

- Ceramides (up to ~50% of total lipids) with cholesterol and free fatty acids (~1:1) are the major lipids of the stratum corneum interstices.
- Lipids are arranged in two lamellar phases (crystalline) with repeat distances of ~6 nm (short) and ~13 nm (long), plus small liquid sub-domains.
- Essential for barrier function: prevent water loss and entry of harmful agents.
- Adipose tissue supplies fatty acids; sebum (waxes, squalene) forms a protective film; antimicrobial peptides/proteins add defence.

## Key facts
- 17 classes of free ceramides and 3 classes of protein-bound ceramides with up to ~1600 molecular species.
- Sphingoid bases: C16–C26 (C18 most abundant) with layer-specific distributions.
- Numerous glucosylceramides based on the same ceramide structures are also essential.
- Non-branched long chains and small head groups → tight packing → low permeability, high barrier efficiency.

## Structural formulae of representative skin ceramides
Diagram labels: 1 Non-hydroxy (type 1); 2 2-Hydroxy ceramides (type 2); 3 ω-O-Acyl ceramides (di-hydroxy base); 4 ω-O-Acyl ceramides (tri-hydroxy base)

- 1: Di-hydroxy base (trans Δ4). R1 = long-chain fatty acid (e.g., C16–C24). R2 = long-chain fatty acid (e.g., C16–C24).
- 2: 2-hydroxy fatty acid (e.g., 2-OH-C16–C24) + dihydroxy base.
- 3: ω-hydroxy very-long-chain fatty acid (n = ~28–30). O-esterified to linoleate (C18:2 n-6). R2 = long-chain FA.
- 4: Trihydroxy base (e.g., phytosphingosine or 6-hydroxy-4-sphingenine). ω-hydroxy VLCFA. O-esterified to linoleate.

R1, R2 = long- or very-long chain (C16–C26) fatty acids (saturated or 2-hydroxy)
R3 = linoleic acid (C18:2 n-6) • n = carbon number of very-long chain (typically 30 or 32)

## Special features
- Distinct structures not seen in other tissues.
- Include ω-O-acyl and 1-O-acylceramides (~5% of total ceramides; up to 700 molecular species in humans/mice).
- Form highly ordered lamellae essential for the epidermal barrier.

## Functions in skin
- Prevent transepidermal water loss and block penetration of xenobiotics, allergens and pathogens.
- Maintain skin hydration, elasticity and mechanical strength.
- Defects → dry skin, atopic dermatitis, ichthyosis.

## 4. CERAMIDES IN HEALTH AND DISEASE

## General principles
- Ceramides are produced rapidly and transiently in response to many stimuli.
- Sources: de novo synthesis, sphingomyelinase pathways, cytoplasmic droplets.
- Promote growth suppression, senescence, cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, migration/adhesion and tumour suppression.
- "Many Ceramides" hypothesis: distinct ceramides in distinct compartments → distinct functions.

## A. Major sources and mechanisms of generation
Sphingomyelinase pathways (rapid: seconds–minutes)
- Acid SMase (lysosomes/PM)
- Neutral SMase (PM, Golgi, ER, mitochondria)
- Alkaline SMase (PM, Golgi)

CELLULAR STRESS
- Oxidative stress
- DNA damage
- Cytokines (TNF-α, FasL)
- Chemotherapy drugs
- UV / ionizing radiation
- Growth factor deprivation
- Inflammation

De novo synthesis pathway (minutes–hours)
- Ceramide synthases (CerS1–6)
- Endoplasmic reticulum

Diagram labels: CERAMIDE

## B. Enzyme activation / deactivation and signalling
- Ceramides activate or inhibit many enzymes: PKCζ, JNK, p38 MAPK, PP2A, etc.
- Inhibit phospholipase D → ↓ phosphatidic acid → alters signalling.
- Alter protein phosphatases (PP2A, PP1) → affect glycogen synthesis, apoptosis.
- Regulate gene expression (NF-κB, p53, FOXO), cell cycle arrest (G0/G1, G2/M), senescence.
- Modulate inflammation, immune responses and membrane microdomains (rafts).

## C. Ceramides and apoptosis
Extrinsic pathway (plasma membrane)

Death ligands (TNF-α, FasL, TRAIL) → bind to death receptors → activates acid SMase → ceramide-rich microdomains (rafts) → receptor clustering → DISC formation → caspase-8 activation → apoptosis

Intrinsic pathway (mitochondria)

Stress (ER → MAMs) → mitochondria → activates acid SMase → ↑ mitochondrial ceramide → outer membrane permeabilization → cytochrome c release → Apaf-1 / caspase-9 activation → caspase-3 / -7 activation → apoptosis

Diagram labels: Ceramide-rich platforms (rafts); DISC

Other ceramide actions
- Promote Bax translocation and inhibit Bcl-2.
- Induce ER stress and Ca2+ release.
- Facilitate autophagy or necroptosis (context-dependent).

## D. Ceramide species and function (chain-length dependent)
| Fatty acyl chain length | Typical roles | Examples | Disease associations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medium (≤ C14) | Membrane structure; Signalling (context-dependent) | C8, C12, C14 | Lysosomal storage (Farber); some neuropathies |
| Long (C16–C18) | Pro-apoptotic; Growth arrest; Metabolic dysfunction | C16 (CerS6), C18 | Cancer, diabetes, obesity, insulin resistance, atherosclerosis |
| Very-long (≥ C20) | Barrier / structural; Other protective; Less inflammatory | C20, C22, C24, C26+ | Skin barrier, myelin integrity, neuro-protection |

## E. Health and disease associations (examples)
| Disease / Condition | Role of ceramides | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Cancer | ↑ Long-chain ceramides | Induce apoptosis, inhibit proliferation; tumour suppressor |
| Metabolic diseases (obesity, diabetes, insulin resistance) | ↑ C16–C18 ceramides | Accumulate in muscle, liver and adipose tissue; correlate with insulin resistance |
| Cardiovascular disease | Pro-atherogenic | Endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, foam cell formation, plaque instability |
| Neurodegenerative diseases | Altered ceramide metabolism | Parkinson's, Alzheimer's, Huntington's; ceramide accumulation in neurons |
| Lysosomal storage diseases | Ceramide accumulation | Farber (acid ceramidase deficiency); Niemann–Pick (ASM deficiency); [illegible] biomarker in Farber |
| Skin disorders | Altered levels or composition | Atopic dermatitis ↓ ceramides; psoriasis, ichthyosis (synthetic/processing defects) |
| Infectious diseases | Modulate pathogen entry | Ceramide-rich domains facilitate many viral infections; high ceramide can inhibit HIV entry |
| Autoimmune / inflammatory | Regulate immune cell function | Influence T-cell activation, cytokine production, macrophage polarization |
| Aging | Ceramide accumulation | Associated with cellular senescence, frailty and chronic disease |

## G. Role of very-long-chain vs long-chain ceramides
- Long-chain ceramides (C16–C18): often harmful in excess (apoptosis, insulin resistance, inflammation).
- Very-long-chain ceramides (≥ C24): usually support structural functions, barrier integrity, neuroprotection and are less inflammatory.

## H. Regulation and therapeutic perspectives
- Levels controlled by: enzymes of synthesis, interconversion (SMases, ceramidases, CerS) and degradation.
- Targeting ceramide pathways shows promise in cancer, metabolic and inflammatory diseases.
- Lifestyle and diet can modulate ceramide metabolism and disease risk.

## Abbreviations
SMase: sphingomyelinase; ASM: acid SMase; nSMase: neutral SMase; aSMase: alkaline SMase
CerS: ceramide synthase; Cer: ceramide; ER: endoplasmic reticulum; PM: plasma membrane
MAMs: mitochondria-associated membranes; DISC: death-inducing signaling complex

## Outlook
Understanding "many ceramides" and their regulation opens new opportunities for precision therapies and for promoting health through diet and lifestyle.

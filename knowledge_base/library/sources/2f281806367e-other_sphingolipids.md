# 5. SPHINGOSINE PHOSPHOCHOLINE (LYSO-SPHINGOMYELIN)

## (untitled panel)
Diagram labels: Sphingosine phosphocholine (SPC, lyso-sphingomyelin)

## Occurrence
- Trace levels in tissues.
- Plasma concentration: ~50–130 nM, associated with HDL.
- Released from activated platelets.
- In skin from sphingomyelin deacylase; implicated in atopic dermatitis.
- Rapidly metabolized in tissues.

## Biological functions
- Multi-functional lipid produced under many physiological and pathological conditions.
- Acts via GPCRs (S1P1–5, GPR12 – low affinity) or as a second messenger → intracellular Ca2+ release.
- Promotes cell proliferation, differentiation and cancer progression (e.g., breast cancer invasion).
- Can be converted to S1P by autotaxin.
- Anti-inflammatory: reduces organ dysfunction from LPS toxins in vivo.
- Elevated in Niemann–Pick disease type C (biomarker).
- Increased in metabolic syndrome; possible pro-inflammatory roles.

## Metabolism and relationships
Diagram labels: Sphingosine phosphocholine; Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P); Autotaxin (LPAAT); Ecto-phosphodiesterase (choline-specific)

## Pathophysiological associations
- Pathogens (e.g., P. aeruginosa) hydrolyse SPC to alter host sphingolipidome → promote infection.
- Involved in cardiovascular regulation.
- Biomarker for Niemann–Pick disease type C and metabolic syndrome.

## Related lipid in Niemann–Pick disease type C
Occurs together with an unusual lipid, now known to be N-palmitoyl-O-phosphocholineserine (lysoSM-509 in older literature).

Diagram labels: N-palmitoyl-O-phosphocholineserine (not a sphingolipid; a lipoamino acid)

## Key points
- SPC (lyso-sphingomyelin) is a potent signalling lipid with diverse roles.
- Can act externally via receptors or internally as a second messenger.
- Converted to S1P by autotaxin; rapidly degraded by phosphodiesterases.
- Anti-inflammatory in some models; pro-cancer effects reported.
- Elevated in Niemann–Pick disease type C and metabolic syndrome.

## Sources and formation
- Formed by deacylation of sphingomyelin via sphingomyelin deacylase.
- Found in skin, heart, vessels, brain, immune cells.
- Released on platelet activation.
- Present in HDL; influences the cardiovascular system.

## Metabolism (tissue)
SPC → (phosphodiesterase) sphingosine + phosphocholine
Sphingosine → S1P → palmitic acid + ethanolamine (▶ lipid metabolism)

## Summary
Sphingosine phosphocholine is a bioactive lysosphingolipid present in plasma (mainly HDL) and tissues at low levels. It influences cardiovascular function, inflammation and cancer, and is a biomarker for certain metabolic and lysosomal storage disorders.

# 6. OTHER SPHINGOLIPIDS CLOSELY RELATED TO SPHINGOMYELIN

## A. 3-O-ACYL-D-ERYTHRO-SPHINGOMYELIN
Present in newborn pig and human infants (not in adults); disappears after ~4 weeks.

An additional fatty acid (C16 or C18) is esterified to the 3-hydroxyl of sphingosine (alkali-labile ester).

- R1 = fatty acid (amide-linked, usually 16:0 or 18:0)
- R2 = fatty acid (ester-linked, usually 16:0 or 18:0)

## B. STRUCTURE OF 3-O-ACYL-SPHINGOMYELIN
- R1: amide-linked fatty acyl chain
- R2: ester-linked fatty acyl chain (at C-3 OH)
- Extra acyl chain is alkali-labile (ester).
- Not detected beyond infancy.

## Other unusual finds
- Cyanobacterium Scytonema julianum: SM with acetyl group esterified to ω-1 hydroxyl of long-chain FA.
- Brown spider venom phospholipase D converts SM to cyclic phosphate (intramolecular transphosphatidylation) → disrupts membranes.
- Earthworms and C. elegans: phosphorylcholine linked to carbohydrate moiety of glycosphingolipids rather than directly to ceramide.

## C. OTHER RELATED SPHINGOLIPIDS
- Bacterial sphingomyelinases (e.g., P. aeruginosa) can act as SM synthases in vitro.
- Pathogens and toxins exploit host sphingolipid metabolism.

# 7. CERAMIDE PHOSPHOETHANOLAMINE AND OTHER SPHINGOPHOSPHOLIPIDS

## A. CERAMIDE PHOSPHOETHANOLAMINE (CPE)
Diagram labels: Sphingolipid analogue of phosphatidylethanolamine
- Occurs in insects, some freshwater invertebrates and certain Bacteroides (gut microbiota).
- Trace levels in most mammalian cells (300–1,500-fold below SM).
- N-methylated by PE N-methyltransferase → sphingomyelin.
- Synthesized in ER (low levels) by SMS1 and SMS2 (bifunctional).
- Major sphingolipid in Drosophila melanogaster (d14:1; d16:1 bases).
- Replaces galactosylceramide in insect "myelin-like" membranes.
- In insects synthesized using CDP-ethanolamine (Kennedy pathway).
- Modulates circadian rhythm via neural–glial coupling in Drosophila.

## Key features of CPE
- Structurally similar to PE but with ceramide backbone.
- Negatively charged head group (zwitterionic at physiological pH).
- High phase transition temperature; does not form rafts with cholesterol.
- Important for membrane organization in insects and some microbes.

## B. OTHER SPHINGOPHOSPHOLIPIDS
1. Ceramide Phosphoglycerol (CPG)
Diagram labels: (n = 12–16)
- Anaerobic Bacteroides spp.; also in P. gingivalis (oral pathogen).
- In P. gingivalis: R1 = 3-hydroxy-iso-methylhexadecanoic acid (amide-linked); esterified to iso-methyltetradecanoic acid (estolide linkage).
- Other relatives: dihydroceramide phosphoglycerol; ceramide phosphoethanolamine.

2. Ceramide Phosphorylmannose (CPMan)
Diagram labels: (n = 12–18)
- Found in Sphingobacterium spiritovorum (iso-branched base and FA). Stimulates murine macrophages via Toll-like receptor.

## Other sphingophospholipids reported
Ceramide phosphoinositol (CPI), ceramide-1-phosphate (Cer-1-P), diphosphoglycerate derivatives, phosphonolipid analogues in some marine invertebrates, protozoa (e.g., Trypanosoma brucei makes SM, CPI, CPE).

Second type: glycosphingolipids further phosphorylated on carbohydrate (earthworms, C. elegans).

## Abbreviations
SPC: sphingosine phosphocholine; S1P: sphingosine-1-phosphate; GPCR: G protein-coupled receptor; HDL: high-density lipoprotein; LPAAT: lysophospholipase D (autotaxin); LPS: lipopolysaccharide; SPC: sphingosine phosphocholine.

## Take-home message
A diverse family of sphingophospholipids related to sphingomyelin exist in animals, microbes and lower eukaryotes. They have unique structures and roles in membranes, signalling and host–pathogen interactions.

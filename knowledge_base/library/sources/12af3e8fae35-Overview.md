# 1. CHOLESTEROL – STRUCTURE AND OCCURRENCE

## (untitled panel)
In animal tissues, cholesterol (or cholest-5-en-3β-ol) is by far the most abundant member of a family of polycyclic lipids known as sterols, which can be classified as polyisoprenoids or triterpenes from their biosynthetic origin. Cholesterol was first recognized as a component of gallstones as long ago as 1769, while the great French lipid chemist Chevreul isolated it from animal fats in 1815. However, it was well into the 20th century before the structure was fully defined by the German Chemist Heinrich Wieland, [illegible], who in 1927, received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work, the first of thirteen so honoured for research on cholesterol and its metabolism.

Cholesterol plays a vital role in animal life as a structural component of membranes, a precursor of steroid hormones and bile acids, and in signalling and transport processes.

## 1. CHOLESTEROL – STRUCTURE AND OCCURRENCE
Structural formula for cholesterol

Diagram labels: A; B; C; D; HO; Cholesterol = cholest-5-en-3β-ol

Molecular formula: C27H46O
Molecular mass: 386.7 g·mol-1

## Key structural features
- Tetracyclic cyclopenta[a]phenanthrene nucleus (rings A–D).
- Trans ring junctions.
- Hydroxyl group at C-3 (β-configuration).
- Double bond between C-5 and C-6.
- Iso-octyl side chain at C-17 (β-oriented) above the plane.
- Two angular methyl groups at C-18 and C-19 (β-oriented).

Numbering system: Fieser–Fieser (1959) IUPAC-IUB system (1989) also used, but Fieser–Fieser preferred by most authors.

## DISTRIBUTION OF CHOLESTEROL IN ANIMAL CELLS
- Site (organelle): Plasma membrane; % of cellular cholesterol*: 60–80%; Features: ~30–50% of lipids in the membrane; Regulates fluidity, permeability, signalling
- Site (organelle): Mitochondria; % of cellular cholesterol*: ~10–15%; Features: Essential for membrane integrity and function
- Site (organelle): Endoplasmic reticulum; % of cellular cholesterol*: ~5%; Features: Lowest proportion
- Site (organelle): Golgi apparatus; % of cellular cholesterol*: ~10–15%; Features: Intermediate amount
- Site (organelle): Early & recycling endosomes; % of cellular cholesterol*: Enriched; Features: Important in trafficking and signalling
- Site (organelle): Late endosomes; % of cellular cholesterol*: Low; Features: Not enriched

*Approximate values; may vary with cell type and species.

## (untitled panel)
BRAIN: contains ~25% of total body free cholesterol. 70–80% of brain cholesterol is in myelin sheath. Major component of synaptic vesicles; controls their shape and function. Precursor of bioactive oxysterols.

## FORMS AND OCCURRENCE
Forms in animal tissues
- Free (unesterified) cholesterol
- Cholesterol esters (with long-chain fatty acids)
- In other covalent linkages (e.g., with proteins)
- In non-covalent aggregates in plasma lipoproteins

Occurrence
- Ubiquitous in all animal tissues (and some fungi).
- Present in every nucleated animal cell.
- Only glucose is present in blood at a higher molar concentration than cholesterol.
- Also occurs in plants (as phytosterols; cholesterol minor component) and some microorganisms.

## Synthesis and dietary sources
- Most animals synthesise high proportion of their cholesterol requirement (de novo synthesis essential even with dietary intake; vital in brain).
- Many invertebrates (insects, crustaceans, some molluscs, nematodes, e.g., C. elegans) cannot synthesise cholesterol and must obtain it from diet.
- Some species can convert plant sterols (e.g., β-sitosterol) to cholesterol.
- Most prokaryotes lack cholesterol; a few pathogens acquire it from hosts; one marine genus can synthesise it de novo.

## VITAL ROLES AND FUNCTIONS OF CHOLESTEROL
- Membrane structure: Regulates membrane fluidity, thickness, stability and permeability.
- Precursor of steroid hormones: Glucocorticoids, oestrogens, progestins, androgens, aldosterone.
- Precursor of bile acids: Essential for lipid digestion and absorption in intestines.
- Cell signalling & development: 'Hedgehog' proteins (cho-linkage) vital for embryonic development.
- Nervous system: Myelin sheath integrity, nerve conduction, synaptic vesicle function.
- Transport: Component of plasma lipoproteins (LDL, HDL) for cholesterol transport.
- Gene transcription: Modulates activity of transcription factors (e.g., SREBP, LXR).
- Reproduction: Essential for hormone synthesis and reproductive processes.
- Stress responses: Involved in cellular stress, inflammation and immune responses.
- Others: Sodium/water balance, Ca2+ & P metabolism, morphogenesis, etc.

## (untitled panel)
Excess cholesterol can be toxic and contributes to diseases such as cardiovascular disease, neurodegenerative disorders and cancer. Therefore, levels must be tightly balanced by synthesis, absorption, conversion and clearance.

## RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER LIPID CLASSES
Diagram labels: Cholesterol esters; ACAT (esterification); CHOLESTEROL; Oxysterols (hydroxylated derivatives); Steroid hormones (Cortisol, oestrogen, testosterone, etc.); Bile acids (Cholic acid, chenodeoxycholic acid, etc.); Vitamin D3 (from 7-dehydro-cholesterol)

Metabolic pathways are tightly regulated to maintain optimal cellular and systemic cholesterol homeostasis.

## DIVERSITY IN NATURE
- Animals: Cholesterol is the predominant sterol.
- Plants: Mixture of phytosterols: β-sitosterol, campesterol, stigmasterol, etc.
- Fungi: Ergosterol and other sterols present.
- Microorganisms: Most lack sterols. Some pathogens acquire cholesterol from hosts.

## WHY CHOLESTEROL IS ESSENTIAL
- Structural integrity of membranes and lipid rafts.
- Precursor of many critical signalling molecules.
- Required for normal development and physiology.
- Supports rapid and precise cellular communication.
- Homeostasis is crucial – both deficiency and excess are harmful.

## CLINICAL RELEVANCE
- High plasma LDL-cholesterol → atherosclerosis, heart disease.
- Altered cholesterol metabolism → neurodegenerative diseases, cancer.
- Therapeutic targets: HMG-CoA reductase (statins), cholesterol absorption (ezetimibe), PCSK9 inhibitors, bile acid sequestrants.
- Research continues to uncover new roles of cholesterol and its metabolites in health and disease.

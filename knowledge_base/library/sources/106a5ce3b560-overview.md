# CARNITINE

## (untitled panel)
CARNITINE: The quaternary ammonium compound and amino acid carnitine (L-3-hydroxy-4-aminobutyrobetaine) and its acyl esters (acylcarnitines) are essential for the mitochondrial transport of long-chain fatty acids for β-oxidation and energy production. They also bind acyl groups from amino acid metabolism (especially branched-chain amino acids) to aid their elimination and help regulate the acyl-CoA/CoA ratio and mitochondrial enzyme activities.

## STRUCTURAL FORMULAE FOR CARNITINE AND ACYLCARNITINES
Diagram labels: L-Carnitine (L-3-hydroxy-4-N-trimethylaminobutanoic acid) (3-hydroxy-4-aminobutyrobetaine); Acylcarnitines (general structure); Homocarnitine (γ-butyrobetaine or 4-trimethylammonio-1-hydroxybutyrate)

L-Carnitine
- Quaternary ammonium betaine (positively charged).
- Exists naturally in the L-configuration.
- Highly water-soluble, zwitterionic at physiological pH.

Acylcarnitines (general structure)
- Formed by esterification of carnitine with acyl-CoA.
- R = acyl group (fatty acyl or other acyl residues).
- Acylcarnitines are more lipophilic than carnitine.

Homocarnitine
- One methylene longer than carnitine.
- Synthesised in mammals by hydroxylation of δ-valerobetaine derived from the microbiome.

## 1. CARNITINE SYNTHESIS AND DIETARY UPTAKE

## 1A. DE NOVO BIOSYNTHESIS IN ANIMALS
Occurs in liver, kidney and brain using N-trimethyl-lysine from protein degradation as the primary precursor.

Diagram labels: Protein degradation; Lysine; Trimethyllysine dioxygenase; N6-Trimethyl-lysine; Trimethyllysine hydroxylase; 3-Hydroxy-N6-trimethyl-lysine; Aldehyde dehydrogenase; γ-Butyrobetaine aldehyde; Aldehyde dehydrogenase; γ-Butyrobetaine (butyrobetaine); γ-Butyrobetaine hydroxylase; L-Carnitine

Total endogenous synthesis ≈ 1.2 μmol/day/kg body weight (insufficient for most individuals).

## 1B. DIETARY UPTAKE AND TRANSPORT
Diagram labels: Intestinal lumen; Enterocyte; Blood (circulation); Target tissue cell; Kidney (proximal tubule); Dietary carnitine; Active transport (OCTN2); Simple diffusion; Acetylation (in some species, e.g., rat, guinea pig); Acetylcarnitine (intracellular); Transported to tissues in blood (bound to plasma components or free); Uptake by specific carnitine transporters (OCTN family); High-affinity reabsorption via OCTN2 in brush border membrane

Major dietary sources: meat, fish, dairy products (2–12 μmol/day/kg body weight for humans)

## KEY POINTS
- Carnitine concentrations in tissues: 0.2–6 mmol/kg.
- Highest levels in heart and skeletal muscle.
- Essential for transport of long-chain fatty acyl groups into mitochondria for β-oxidation.
- Binds acyl groups from amino acid metabolism (particularly branched-chain amino acids) → facilitates excretion.
- Regulates the acyl-CoA/CoA ratio and activity of several mitochondrial enzymes.

## RENAL HANDLING
- High-affinity reabsorption of carnitine and butyrobetaine by OCTN2 transporter.
- Minimizes urinary loss; excess amounts are eliminated when necessary.

## NOTE
D-Carnitine does not occur naturally. It does not participate in key biological processes and can interfere with them.

## Abbreviations
CoA, coenzyme A; OCTN, organic cation transporter novel; β-oxidation, mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation; acylcarnitines, esters of carnitine with acyl groups.

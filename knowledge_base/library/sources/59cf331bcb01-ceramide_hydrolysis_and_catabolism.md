# 2. CERAMIDE HYDROLYSIS AND CATABOLISM

## (untitled panel)
Ceramides are hydrolysed by ceramidases to sphingoid bases and free fatty acids. This is the only route to unesterified sphingosine. Sphingoid bases can be re-used for ceramide resynthesis (salvage pathway) or converted to other signalling lipids.

## A. Ceramide hydrolysis and resynthesis (salvage pathway)

Other fates of sphingoid bases
- Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P)
- Ceramide-1-phosphate (C1P)
- Sphingoid base phosphates (e.g., SphK2)
- N-Acylation (recycling)
- Export from lysosome to cytosol

## B. Ceramidases in humans
| Enzyme (Other name) | pH optimum | Cellular location | Preferred acyl chain length (substrate preference) | Notes / Functions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acid ceramidase (ASAH1) | ~4.5 | Lysosomes | C6 – C18 (short to medium) | Key in lysosomal ceramide degradation. Deficiency → Farber disease (↑ ceramide). Regulates epidermal ceramide levels/composition. |
| Neutral ceramidase (ASAH2) | ~7 | Plasma membrane, Golgi, (Intestinal epithelial cells, colon) | C16 – C18 (long chain) | Can work in reverse (synthesis) e.g., in mitochondria. Dietary ceramides (lumen) hydrolysed here. May contribute to ceramide synthesis in mitochondria. |
| Alkaline ceramidase 1 (ACER1) | ~9 | Endoplasmic reticulum (also plasma mem./ mitochondria) | Very-long chain (C20 – C26+) | Highly expressed in skin. Prefers very-long-chain acyl ceramides. |
| Alkaline ceramidase 2 (ACER2) | ~9 | Golgi apparatus (also mitochondria) | Very-long chain (C20 – C26+) | Found in placenta, pancreas, heart. Prefers very-long-chain acyl ceramides. |
| Alkaline ceramidase 3 (ACER3) | ~9 | ER and Golgi (widespread; also nucleus) | Unsaturated long chain (18:1, 20:1, 20:4) (also dihydroceramides) | Broad tissue distribution (esp, placenta). Acts on unsaturated ceramides, dihydroceramides, phytoceramides. |

Sphingoid bases released from lysosomes can be exported to the cytosol and re-acylated by ceramide synthases. The salvage pathway is estimated to contribute 50-90% of sphingolipid biosynthesis.

## C. Plants and lower organisms
- Arabidopsis has alkaline ceramidase (AtACER) and several neutral ceramidases (AtNCERs).
- No equivalent acid ceramidase identified in plants.
- Rice enzyme prefers d18:1Δ4-ceramide; can operate in reverse to increase C26, C28 phytoceramides.
- Ceramidases occur in Pseudomonas aeruginosa and slime moulds (secreted proteins).
- Most prokaryotes have a neutral ceramidase only, including some pathogenic bacteria.

## KEY POINTS (SUMMARY)
- Ceramidases are the only enzymes that release free sphingoid bases from ceramides.
- Five human ceramidases differ in pH optimum, location, and acyl-chain preference.
- Salvage pathway recycles sphingoid bases and supplies the majority of sphingolipid synthesis.
- Ceramides have unique physical properties that shape membranes and enable signalling.
- Dysregulation of ceramide metabolism contributes to many human diseases.

## Clinical relevance examples
- Farber disease: acid ceramidase deficiency → ceramide storage (↑ C26:0).
- Metabolic syndrome, diabetes, cancer: altered ceramide species and levels.
- Skin barrier defects: abnormal ceramide composition (e.g., in atopic dermatitis).
- Neurodegeneration: ceramide accumulation and imbalanced signalling.

## 3. PHYSICAL PROPERTIES OF CERAMIDES

## A. Molecular shape and membrane effects
- Cone-shaped (small head group + bulky tails).
- Short-chain ceramides → positive curvature; long-chain ceramides → negative curvature.
- Favour non-bilayer (inverted hexagonal) phases and membrane fusion/invagination.
- Increase acyl chain order in bilayers.
- Affect ion channels → increase membrane permeability to solutes (small to protein size).
- Required for formation and secretion of exosomes.

## B. Ceramide-rich domains ("ceramide platforms")
Diagram labels: Extracellular; Cytosol; Ceramide; Cholesterol; Sphingomyelin; Other lipids

- Ceramides concentrate in lateral liquid-ordered microdomains (ceramide platforms/rafts).
- Can displace cholesterol (C12–C18 ceramides).
- Generated by acid sphingomyelinase in rafts.
- Promote raft coalescence and clustering of receptors and signalling proteins.
- Amplify receptor- and stress-mediated signalling.

## C. Signalling and physiological roles
- Ceramide-rich domains recruit receptors (e.g., death receptors, cytokine receptors) and signalling complexes.
- Facilitate oligomerization of death receptors → apoptosis.
- In endothelial cells: peptide (CGSPGWVRC) binds C16-ceramide → activates ASM without apoptotic signalling.
- Sites for endocytic uptake of pathogens (concentrates receptors); enhances infection by Norovirus, JEV, Ebola, SARS-CoV-2, etc.
- Elevated ceramide inhibits HIV entry.
- Ceramide-rich domains initiate and amplify primary signals while excluding inhibitory signals.

Diagram labels: Signal amplification

## D. Comparison with other lipids
- Similar in structure to diacylglycerols but different in location and behaviour.
- Cross synthetic bilayers relatively quickly in vitro.
- Flip-flop in complex membranes in vivo may be restricted within ceramide-rich domains → opposite leaflets may have different ceramide pools and functions.

## E. Factors influencing ceramide behaviour
- Sphingoid base unsaturation → stronger intramolecular H-bonding → tight packing.
- Fatty acyl chain length → curvature, membrane order, phase behaviour.
- Chain composition → microdomain preference and protein interactions.

## 4. CATABOLISM AND REGULATION

## A. Catabolism
- Ceramidases hydrolyse ceramide → sphingoid base + fatty acid.
- Acid ceramidase active in lysosomes (C6–C18).
- Neutral/alkaline ceramidases act at plasma membrane, Golgi, ER, mitochondria, nucleus (tissue- and substrate-specific).
- Lipases and sphingomyelinases provide ceramide precursors.
- Catabolism regulated by pH, cofactors, localization, and protein partners.

## B. Biological significance
- Balance of ceramide synthesis (de novo vs salvage) and hydrolysis controls cellular ceramide levels.
- Regulates apoptosis, proliferation, inflammation, stress responses, and lysosomal function.
- Aberrant ceramidase activity or ceramide accumulation linked to cancer, metabolic diseases, neurodegeneration, lysosomal storage disorders, and skin diseases.
